Step 1

Getting an evaluation & an IEP (Committee on Special Education)

Any NYC child can be referred to their school's Committee on Special Education (CSE) for a free evaluation to determine whether they qualify for an Individualized Education Program (IEP) and specialized school placement.

    The state's overview of how the Committee on Special Education process and IEP development work.

  • NYC DOE: Committee on Special Education (CSE) Official · NYC DOE

  • NYC DOE: Requesting a Special Education Evaluation Official · NYC DOE

    Step-by-step instructions for formally requesting an evaluation in writing.

  • NYC DOE: The IEP Process: Evaluation Official · NYC DOE

    Where evaluation fits into the overall IEP timeline.

  • NYC DOE: Special Education Standard Operating Procedures Manual (SOPM) Official · NYC DOE · Reference manual

    DOE's own internal procedures manual for special education: the detailed rulebook that lays out exactly what schools and CSEs are required to do, and by when, at every stage: referral, evaluation timelines, IEP development and annual review, reevaluation, related services, and placement. It's written for DOE staff, not families, so it's dense; but because it documents the DOE's own required procedures, it's a useful reference if a school seems to be skipping steps or missing timelines, including in a reimbursement or impartial hearing case (see Evaluations, Sub-category 4).

Step 2

DOE public school special education programs

Most NYC students with IEPs are served in specialized public-school settings: free, since they're part of the DOE system.

  • NYC DOE: Specialized Programs for Students with Disabilities Official · NYC DOE · Free

    Overview of DOE special-education school settings, including District 75 programs for students with more intensive needs.

  • NYC Autism Charter School Free · Public charter · Lottery

    A tuition-free public charter school specifically for students with autism, in the Bronx and East Harlem. Admission is by application/lottery, not a CSE placement; see their Apply page.

  • NYC DOE: Autism (ASD) Family Resource Guide Official · NYC DOE · Free

    Explains the two DOE autism-specific program models: ASD Nest (integrated co-teaching classes, partnered with NYU, for students with average-to-above-average academic skills) and ASD Horizon (small self-contained classes, max. 8 students, for students needing more support). Per this guide, the DOE does not publish a current site list and does not accept requests for a specific school: "student applications are assigned a specific school." See also the NYLPI Nest & Horizon factsheet for a plain-language comparison, and ask your CSE how to apply for either program.

  • Current program size & how to get your child's actual site Citywide totals · Jan 2025

    Neither program publishes a school-by-school site list, but DOE gave the Council these citywide figures on January 30, 2025: ASD Nest: 88 schools, 442 classes, 1,972 students, grades K–12; ASD Horizon: 69 schools, 255 classes, 1,853 students, grades K–12. Both programs have also expanded in recent years (e.g., 160 new seats added in January 2024, concentrated in Districts 5, 12, and 14) so the site mix keeps shifting. To find out which specific school your own child would be assigned to, email ASDPrograms@schools.nyc.gov or ask your CSE directly: DOE will only confirm a site in the context of an actual application.

Step 3

State-funded / approved schools ("853" & "4410" schools)

When a public-school setting isn't appropriate, a CSE can place a student at a State-approved nonpublic school; often called "853 schools" (school-age) or "4410 schools" (preschool). These are privately run but publicly funded, so families generally pay no tuition once the CSE places the child there.

Step 5

Manhattan: list of private specialized schools

A parent-crowdsourced, informal list of Manhattan-area private (and a few state-funded) special education schools, grouped into rough tiers from fewer to more supports, based on notes from schools themselves, parent feedback in a community forum, and follow-up research. This is not an official or vetted ranking: it reflects one point-in-time attempt to compare options, isn't a comment on any child's potential, and the tier a school sits in below is a loose approximation, not a formal classification. Use it only as a starting point for your own research, and confirm everything directly with each school.

Each school name below is also tagged Funded if NYSED has approved it as a school districts can contract with (meaning a CBST/CSE placement can be tuition-free), or Nonfunded if it has no such approval (meaning families generally pay tuition directly and, in many cases, separately pursue reimbursement through an impartial hearing). This was cross-checked directly against NYSED's own "Listings of New York State Approved Private Schools" spreadsheet (the official 853/state-supported school roster), supplemented by State Review Officer (SRO) impartial-hearing decisions and each school's own site; approval status can change over time and some "Nonfunded" schools may still have limited funded seats, so confirm current status directly with the school and your CSE/CBST caseworker before relying on it for a placement decision.

Among NYC parents and private schools, the reimbursement route referenced above is commonly discussed under two colloquial labels, Carter funding and Connors funding, both grounded in case law rather than any formal DOE program. Carter funding (named for the underlying legal standard from School Committee of the Town of Burlington v. Department of Education of Massachusetts (1985) and Florence County School District Four v. Carter (1993)) is retroactive: a parent pays tuition out of pocket, then seeks reimbursement through an impartial hearing by showing the district failed to offer an appropriate program (denying a free appropriate public education), that the chosen private placement was appropriate for the child even though it need not meet every state education standard, and that the equities favor the parent. Connors funding (named for Connors v. Mills, a U.S. District Court case) is prospective: a family that cannot pay tuition upfront persuades a non-approved school to enroll the child on credit, commits to pursuing the same impartial-hearing showing, and, if the case succeeds, has the district begin paying the school directly going forward rather than reimbursing the family after the fact; schools willing to offer this kind of seat on credit are limited, since payment depends on the outcome of litigation or settlement. In both cases, parents generally must give the Committee on Special Education prior written notice (at least 10 school days) before removing the child and enrolling privately, or risk losing reimbursement rights; this is the legal basis on which families of children at "Nonfunded" schools most often seek public funds, it is decided case by case rather than guaranteed, so consult an education attorney or advocate (see Education Attorneys & Advocates) before relying on it.

  • OPWDD: Determining Eligibility for Services (detailed guidelines PDF) Official · NY State

    The full, detailed eligibility standard. New York's Mental Hygiene Law requires all four of: (1) a qualifying developmental disability, (2) onset before age 22, (3) expected to continue indefinitely, and (4) a substantial handicap to functioning. The documentation package OPWDD asks for to determine eligibility typically includes: a psychological report within 3 years including an IQ assessment (find a provider via OPWDD's assessment-provider finder); an adaptive-behavior assessment (interpretive report with summary/composite/domain scores if IQ is above 60, or one based on caregiver interviews, records, and observation if IQ is below 60; an Early Intervention Multidisciplinary Core Evaluation can work for young children if it includes standardized cognitive/language/adaptive/social/motor scores); a general medical report from within the past 365 days; a social/developmental history or psychosocial report showing disability onset before age 22; and a social evaluation from within the past 365 days prepared by a Care Manager/supervisor, QIDP, LMSW/LCSW, psychologist, or someone with a Master's in psychology who knows the individual.

  • Common qualifying pathways, in practice Practical · Reported by families

    In practice, two documented pathways account for most successful applications: a full-scale IQ score below 70 on standardized psychological testing, or, for applicants qualifying on the basis of an autism spectrum diagnosis, a documented Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule (ADOS) score. A CPSE (Committee on Preschool Special Education) evaluation can also satisfy the evaluation requirement for younger children. Families report that applications tend to proceed more smoothly when at least one submitted evaluation documents specific behavioral concerns, so it is worth confirming that this is reflected where applicable. Processing an eligibility application typically takes about two to two and a half months.

Step 3

  • Ways to apply for Medicaid Practical · Multiple options

    If you're not already enrolled, you can apply: directly through your county Medicaid office; through NY State of Health at 1-855-355-5777; through a community services agency you already work with (many can help complete the application and explain required documents); or once OPWDD's Front Door refers you to a Care Coordination Organization, which will assist with Medicaid enrollment.   • Medicaid's special provisions for developmental disabilities Good to know

    A few Medicaid rules apply specifically to people with developmental disabilities: individuals can work and still qualify for Medicaid, though depending on income they may need to contribute to service costs; parental income and resources can be waived (not counted) for children living at home who are seeking enrollment in the OPWDD HCBS Waiver program; and financial resources can be placed in a Medicaid-qualifying supplemental needs trust and remain exempt for Medicaid purposes.   • The Waiver approval and the Notice of Decision Practical · Reported by families

    Once the HCBS Waiver application, including any requested Respite care for younger children, is approved, OPWDD issues a Notice of Decision, referred to by families as the "NOD-09," listing the specific service types and hours authorized. This approval is a prerequisite for starting Self-Direction. Families must also attend an OPWDD Self-Direction information session, currently held over WebEx, before proceeding. This stage typically takes about one to two months.

  • Choosing a Support Broker and Fiscal Intermediary Practical · Reported by families

    Once Waiver services are approved, the next step is selecting a Support Broker and a Fiscal Intermediary (FI), the entity that manages payroll and reimbursements for a Self-Direction budget; some brokers work with a specific FI.   • From start-up budget to final approval Practical · Reported by families

  • How Self-Direction is actually funded Good to know

    Self-Directed services are paid for out of a Personal Resource Account (PRA): an individualized budget amount based on the person's assessed needs. The PRA can cover things like respite, community habilitation, classes, memberships, and transportation, but it comes with real record-keeping and administrative work (tracking spending, paying a Fiscal Intermediary, documenting services). Step 7

Benefits: Medicaid, OPWDD, and other programs

An overview of benefits families report using once Medicaid and/or OPWDD services are in place. This list is not exhaustive, and the specifics below reflect families' reported experience rather than official policy in every case; confirm current amounts, eligibility, and application steps directly with the relevant agency before relying on them.

Medicaid benefits
  • Managed Medicaid vs. straight Medicaid Good to know

    Managed Medicaid functions as a form of health insurance and is useful for families without commercial coverage. Straight (fee-for-service) Medicaid is accepted by fewer providers, but where a provider accepts both, it can serve as a secondary payer covering copayments and coinsurance on a primary commercial plan.

  • Home Health Aide (HHA) hours Good to know

    Medicaid can fund a home health aide. Families with Managed Medicaid generally report being able to bill more aide hours; those with straight Medicaid typically need to work through a Medicaid-network home-health-aide agency, or have the aide's hours billed to the primary commercial insurer and denied before Medicaid pays as secondary. Because Medicaid tends to authorize more hours at better rates than commercial insurance, some families find it advantageous to apply early in the calendar year, before the primary insurance deductible has been met. The Consumer Directed Personal Assistance Program (CDPAP) works similarly to a home health aide but allows a relative or friend to serve as the aide without the background check, fingerprinting, or HHA certification an agency aide would require.

  • OT, speech, and PT through Medicaid Reported by families

    Medicaid covers occupational therapy, speech therapy, and physical therapy. Families report mixed experiences with availability and continuity of care under Medicaid's reimbursement rates, which are generally lower than commercial insurance, though this varies by provider and location.

  • Con Edison & National Grid: Energy Affordability Program (HEAP) Official · NY State

    Income-qualifying households, including many Medicaid households, can receive a discount on monthly electric and/or gas bills through New York's Energy Affordability Program, administered through HEAP (the Home Energy Assistance Program) and offered by Con Edison, National Grid, and other utilities statewide. Families have reported discounts in the range of $40 per month on Con Edison bills, though the actual amount depends on income, household size, and utility territory; apply directly with your utility or through HEAP.

  • Verizon: Lifeline discount Official · Multi-state

    Verizon's Lifeline program offers a reduced monthly rate on home phone and Fios internet service to income-qualifying households, including those enrolled in Medicaid, SNAP, SSI, or certain other assistance programs. Families have reported discounts of about $30 per month; only one Lifeline discount is allowed per household.

  • Reduced-fare MetroCard for an accompanying adult Reported by families

    An adult traveling with a Medicaid-enrolled child who has a disability may qualify for a reduced-fare MetroCard when accompanying the child. Confirm current eligibility and documentation requirements with the MTA.

  • Access-A-Ride Official · NYC

    The MTA's paratransit service operates across all five boroughs and can register up to five family members under one case. An in-person or on-site assessment is required to establish that the individual cannot use the bus or subway independently.

OPWDD: Traditional (HCBS) services
  • Respite HCBS · Traditional service

    Provides caregiving coverage, similar in practice to hiring a babysitter with disability experience. Families report a typical rate around $15 per hour; a holiday, site-based respite option may also be available.

  • Community Habilitation (Con-Hab) HCBS · Traditional service

    Pays a higher hourly rate than respite, but is generally not authorized for children under age 8.

  • Family Support Services (FSS) reimbursement Bridge to Self-Direction

    Reimburses up to roughly $3,000 per year for classes and related expenses. FSS is technically not an HCBS Waiver service: any individual with OPWDD eligibility can apply regardless of Waiver or Self-Direction status, though the application requires substantial supporting documentation. See OPWDD-related providers above for specific FSS-administering agencies.

  • Day Habilitation, Pre-Vocational Services, Pathway to Employment, and SEMP HCBS · Post-school transition

    Available once a young adult graduates from the school system, supporting day programming, prevocational skill-building, and Supported Employment (SEMP).

OPWDD: Self-Direction budget categories
  • What a Self-Direction budget can cover Good to know

    Self-Direction budgets can be used flexibly across many categories, most commonly: community classes and memberships; travel expenses such as rideshare fares and mileage reimbursement; camp tuition; self-hired respite care, which cannot be used concurrently with HHA or CDPAP hours; Community Habilitation for children over age 8; a housing subsidy for older individuals; Supported Employment (SEMP); and goods or services purchased directly from a provider of the family's choosing, including continuing existing traditional services if preferred.

Other discounts & access programs
  • Disability parking permit Reported by families

    A disability parking permit is available, though approval is not guaranteed for every OPWDD-enrolled individual; it is reportedly granted more consistently to individuals considered medically fragile. Apply through your county clerk's office or the NYC Department of Transportation.

  • Disney and amusement park access programs Reported by families

    Disney, through its Disability Access Service, and many amusement parks offer accommodations such as reduced wait times for guests with disabilities. Policies and documentation requirements vary by venue and change periodically, so confirm current requirements directly with each park before visiting.
